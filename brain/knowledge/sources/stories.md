### Achievements in depth

#### Turning around the Partnerships team (Xepelin, 2024–2025)
- Santiago moved from Portfolio Strategy to lead a newly formed Partnerships area, created by merging three teams. One of them, serving SMB financial institutions that resold Xepelin's platform, had a poor track record and low morale, and it was half of the area's revenue.
- He formed hypotheses from his portfolio background but didn't act on them yet. He held one-on-ones with every team member, talked with the internal functions, and called partners directly, because the data showed what was happening but not why.
- What he found: partners were frustrated by slowness, by how they were treated and by how their private information was handled. They wanted fast financing, a real relationship and their own customers kept happy. Internally, the team was seen as slow and operationally behind.
- He came back with a three-workstream plan (operational organization, product enhancement, acquisition workforce), got the country manager's backing, and then built it with the team so they would own it.
- Results: annual net revenue up 150%, from about five hundred thousand to 1.25 million dollars. 4x monthly active users through API integrations and a self-serve partner portal. The area grew from 6% to 9% of company net revenue. Time-to-active fell 40%, operations time 25%, and overhead 30% by consolidating into one 8-person org. Morale recovered because people felt heard and had visibility.
- His takeaway: bringing the team and the ecosystem into the strategy beats arriving with the answer.

#### Shared credit-line prioritization (Xepelin, 2024)
- In supply chain finance, payors need a credit line, and clients across 8 to 10 teams shared the same large payors. Those lines were allocated first come, first served, not by profit, relationship value or churn risk.
- He took a framework to his manager and to the owning team, RevOps. When they couldn't prioritize it for about six months, he offered to build the first version himself. He spoke with every stakeholder first, built the allocation logic on client lifetime value and expected gross profit, iterated with the teams, and handed it back to RevOps with the same rules for everyone.
- Results: about 65 thousand dollars of extra net revenue in the first month (roughly 700 thousand annualized), and about 450 thousand dollars in total uplift. The tool is still in use.
- His takeaway: a tool built with its users gets adopted; the job description is a floor, not a ceiling.

#### Mortgage product for subsidized housing (Grupo Consorcio, 2021)
- The real estate unit's revenue had been flat for a long time. He found a new growth source in state-subsidized housing, an underserved segment.
- He modeled the product and led it end to end with Product, Engineering, Risk and Legal, bringing Risk and Legal in at the design stage, and presented it directly to the board.
- Result: an addressable market expansion of about 800 million dollars a year.
- His takeaway: in financial services, Risk and Legal are co-designers, not a final approval step.

#### A large bank partnership (Xepelin)
- He led one of Xepelin's most important partnerships, with a large bank. It was scoped at three months and ran twelve.
- He kept the deal's impact clear inside the company, gave stakeholders constant visibility, and decided each round with the client which new requests to absorb and which to push back on.
- Result: it closed, and fully deployed it represented around 15% of the company's net revenue.
- His takeaway: in long deals, the real work is sustaining your own organization's attention and energy.

#### Provisions and risk pricing, and scaling the portfolio (Xepelin, 2021–2024)
- The company was growing fast without a data-backed view of expected default by payor type, and that view was needed to clear the audits for its 110 million dollar Series B.
- He built Xepelin's first provisions and invoice-level risk pricing system from scratch, plus profitability frameworks by risk-and-return cluster. He built and led a three-person portfolio strategy team and ran a biweekly performance committee with the C-level.
- Results: the model cleared the funding round's audit; the portfolio grew from 84 to 267 million dollars, including the Mexico launch; and his portfolio management rules are still in use.
- His takeaway: volume lies; without properly allocated risk cost, a segment that looks good can be losing money.

#### Pricing strategy for an arena move (Sports & Entertainment Advisors, 2026)
- A client was moving into an arena with about three times its previous capacity, so its pricing and product structure no longer made sense.
- He rebuilt pricing and product strategy from the inventory up, optimizing the seat and product mix and benchmarking peer franchises to calibrate each tier.
- Result: about 10 million dollars of incremental annual revenue, roughly 100% growth.
- His takeaway: when capacity changes, redesign the product architecture first and price second.

#### Renewal risk, sales sizing and an AI roadmap (Sports & Entertainment Advisors, 2026)
- He built an automated model scoring non-renewal risk for every season-ticket account. It focused rep effort on the roughly 40% of accounts carrying about 60% of renewal revenue.
- He modeled staffing against the growth target, which doubled the recommended sales headcount, and redesigned commissions to align reps with revenue goals while keeping payroll flat as a share of revenue.
- He ranked about five recurring firm workflows by frequency, risk and value, built the top-ranked automation (client deliverable production), and trained the team on it.
- His takeaway: deciding where commercial effort goes is worth as much as adding more of it.

#### Agora Medios (co-founder and COO, 2014–2019, alongside his degree)
- A student events company serving schools and universities in Chile. He owned client management, closing deals that were deliverable and held margin, and operations: security, crowd control, vendors, ticketing, sponsors and production.
- Result: over 1 million dollars in annual revenue and roughly 80% share of its segment.

#### Robotics World Cup (age 14)
- His school team qualified from Chile's regional championship for the world championship in St. Louis, with one month to prepare across robot building, task performance and a science project.
- He proposed splitting into specializations while still helping across lanes. He took the robot and task solving and helped with the English presentation.
- Result: the team finished in the world's top 15.

### Weaknesses, in his own words

When asked about a weakness, these are the two Santiago names himself, along with what he changed:
- **Starting with the day-to-day instead of the structure.** He has a natural bias for action over planning. When he took over Partnerships at Xepelin he was putting out small fires instead of finding the root cause, and his manager told him so directly. He stopped for two full days to structure the problem, build the data sources and rank the issues by impact, then went back to the day-to-day starting with the 20% that drove 80% of the outcome. He now writes things down and prioritizes explicitly before executing. His lesson: starting slower with structure is faster.
- **Sharing context downward.** He communicates well up and across, but used to under-share with his own team what was happening outside their area, and without that context people make worse calls. At Haas he took the communications role on his MBA study team specifically to practice pushing information down. His lesson: information has to flow both ways, or you become the bottleneck.

### Failures and what he learned

#### The digital mortgage pre-approval (Grupo Consorcio, 2021)
- He led the build of Chile's first digital mortgage pre-approval with design, product, engineering and legal. The whole team, him included, was sure it knew what customers needed, and nobody asked them.
- After three months of development, the real estate companies meant to host it gave it a lukewarm reception. He owned the mistake, admitted it quickly instead of defending the product, and gathered customer input: about 80% of what was missing turned out to be small to medium changes. He took his manager the scenarios, including killing it.
- Result: one more month of development, more than 10 real estate companies using it within two months, and approvals that went from two days to instant.
- His lesson: never make decisions on a customer-facing product without the customer, and require external validation before committing development.

#### Staying too small (Agora Medios)
- Agora dominated school and university events in Chile, but the niche wasn't big enough for three partners full time. They were slow to expand into adjacent markets or run their own events.
- When Covid cancelled every event, there was no diversification to absorb it, and the company closed.
- His lesson: serving your current customer is the floor, not the ceiling; he now asks early how a model scales beyond its first niche.

#### Taking direct feedback
- This is the same story as the first weakness: his manager's feedback that he was firefighting. He took it without getting defensive, restructured his approach, and that work led to the 150% revenue growth and 4x active users.
